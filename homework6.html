1. Theoretical Foundations of Statistical Independence

Given a bivariate dataset of $N$ paired observations $(X_k, Y_k)$ where $X$ takes $r$ distinct categorical values $\{x_1, \dots, x_r\}$ and $Y$ takes $c$ distinct values $\{y_1, \dots, y_c\}$, we summarize their joint behavior in an $r \times c$ contingency table.

  • Joint & Marginal Frequencies: Let $O_{ij}$ be the observed absolute frequency of pair $(x_i, y_j)$. The row marginal is $R_i = \sum_{j=1}^c O_{ij}$ and column marginal is $C_j = \sum_{i=1}^r O_{ij}$, with $\sum_{i,j} O_{ij} = N$.
  • Conditional Frequencies: The empirical conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X = x_i$ is $f(y_j \mid x_i) = \frac{O_{ij}}{R_i}$.
  • Stochastic Independence: Two discrete random variables $X$ and $Y$ are statistically independent if and only if their joint probability factorizes into the product of their marginals: $$P(X = x_i, Y = y_j) = P(X = x_i) \cdot P(Y = y_j) \iff P(Y = y_j \mid X = x_i) = P(Y = y_j) \quad \forall i, j$$

Expected Frequencies & Pearson's Chi-Square Statistic

Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ of independence, the theoretical expected frequency $E_{ij}$ for cell $(i, j)$ is: $$E_{ij} = N \cdot \left(\frac{R_i}{N}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{C_j}{N}\right) = \frac{R_i \cdot C_j}{N}$$ To measure the discrepancy between observed data and $H_0$, we compute Pearson's Chi-Square ($\chi^2$) statistic: $$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^r \sum_{j=1}^c \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$ As $N \to \infty$, under $H_0$, $\chi^2$ converges in distribution to a $\chi^2_\nu$ random variable with $\nu = (r - 1)(c - 1)$ degrees of freedom.

3. Interpretation: Statistical Association vs. Causality

  • Decision Rule: If the computed p-value is smaller than the significance level $\alpha$ (equivalently, $\chi^2_{\text{obs}} > \chi^2_{\text{crit}}$), we reject $H_0$ and conclude that $X$ and $Y$ are statistically dependent. Otherwise, the observed deviations from $E_{ij}$ are consistent with random sampling fluctuations.
  • Association Does Not Imply Causation: Finding statistical dependence ($\chi^2$ significant) establishes a structural correlation between $X$ and $Y$—meaning knowing $X$ alters the conditional probability distribution of $Y$. However, observational contingency tables cannot prove that $X$ directly causes $Y$:
    • Confounding Variables (Lurking Factors): A third unobserved variable $Z$ may drive both $X$ and $Y$ simultaneously (e.g., high network traffic $Z$ causing both elevated firewall alerts $X$ and server latency $Y$).
    • Reverse Causality & Selection Bias: Contingency tables are symmetric ($\chi^2(X,Y) = \chi^2(Y,X)$) and carry no directional or temporal information.
